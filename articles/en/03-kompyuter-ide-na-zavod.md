# The Computer Goes to the Factory: How the "Dnipro" Learned to Run Machines

> **Where we are on the timeline:** 1958–1975. Before this: the story of MESM and the people who built it (articles 1 and 2). Next: computers for a single engineer (article 4).

The first computers were a real wonder, but a wonder that existed in a single copy. Picture it: the machine fills a whole room, a team of scientists keeps watch beside it, and there is no second one like it. What if you made a computer that a factory could turn out by the hundreds? And one that would not just calculate, but also run other machines by itself? That is exactly what the "Dnipro" became.

## Calculate or control?

Back then computers were called EOM — "electronic computing machines". The first ones mostly calculated: scientists gave them a hard problem and waited for the answer.

A computer that controls works differently. It keeps an eye on equipment and gives it commands by itself: switch on, stop, change speed. That is the kind of computer a factory needs.

## An idea born at a conference

In June 1958, at a conference in Kyiv, Viktor Glushkov proposed building a universal computer for control. In other words, one that could be adapted to many different kinds of production.

By then Glushkov was already heading Sergey Lebedev's former lab. And from December 1957 he also led the new Computing Centre of the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences.

The chief designer of the "Dnipro" was Boris Malinovsky. Later he also wrote books on the history of computers in Ukraine — and much of what we know today comes from them.

## From drawings to the factory

A little over three years passed from the idea to the finished machine. On 9 December 1961, a state commission approved the "Dnipro" for factory production.

A dedicated plant was built for it in Kyiv — the Kyiv OKM plant, later known as "Elektronmash". It was built at the same time as the "Dnipro" itself was being developed. From 1962 to 1970, the plant turned out the machines by the hundreds rather than one at a time.

Over ten years, more than 500 "Dnipros" were made. Most of them worked outside Ukraine — mainly in Russia.

## Smaller, but faster

Compare the "Dnipro" with Kyiv's first machine, MESM. MESM took up 60 square metres and used 25 kilowatts. The "Dnipro" needed 35–40 square metres and 4 kilowatts.

The secret lay in tiny parts that replaced the big, hot glass tubes. Thanks to them, the "Dnipro" could perform up to 50,000 operations a second when controlling equipment. MESM managed just 50.

## The "Dnipro" and space

In 1975 came the Soyuz–Apollo flight. It was a joint flight of Soviet and American spacecraft.

Two "Dnipros" were working at the Mission Control Centre at the time. They ran the large screen that the specialists used to follow the flight. A machine designed for factories became part of space history.

## Sievierodonetsk: another centre

Computers were not made only in Kyiv. In Sievierodonetsk, an enterprise that later became known as "Impuls" had been working since 1956. From 1963 it produced the "Promin" machine, which we talk about in article 4. And at the turn of the 1960s and 1970s, "Impuls" created the M-6000 system — more than 18,000 of these systems were produced over ten years.

Glushkov had an even bolder dream: to link the computers of the whole country into a single system. Read about it, and about why it never came true, in article 6.

## Fun fact

According to Glushkov, a similar American machine went into production in June 1961 — "at the same time as ours". So the "Dnipro" was the first in the USSR, but not the first in the world.

## Bridge to today

Look around you: a washing machine decides by itself when to fill with water, and a traffic light decides when to change. They are distant relatives of the idea the "Dnipro" brought to life.

## Bridge to memories

Did anyone in your family work at "Elektronmash", at "Impuls" or at another research institute? Ask them — and share their memories with the museum.

## Talk about it at home

- What could happen at a factory if the computer made a mistake?
- Which devices in your home "decide" things by themselves?
- Why do you think a factory computer had to be smaller and more reliable than the first machines?
