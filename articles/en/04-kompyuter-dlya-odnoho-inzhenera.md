# A Computer for a Single Engineer: the "Promin" and the MIR

> **Where we are on the timeline:** 1963–1970s. Before this: how the "Dnipro" went to the factory (article 3). Next: how a machine was taught to understand people (article 5).

The first computers were huge, and whole teams of specialists worked with them. An ordinary engineer designing a bridge or a machine tool could hardly get near one. What if you built a computer that one person could work with alone? That is what people in Kyiv dreamed of in the 1960s.

## Glushkov's idea

Back then computers were called EOM — "electronic computing machines". Viktor Glushkov, head of the Kyiv Computing Centre and later of the Institute of Cybernetics, believed that an engineer needed a machine of their own. Not for big scientific problems, but for everyday calculations.

Such a machine had to be simple. A person should be able to "talk" to it comfortably, without long training.

## The "Promin": a machine made in eight months

The first step was the "Promin" — a small computer for engineering calculations. Its chief designer was Solomon Pogrebinsky, and its scientific director was Glushkov.

According to Glushkov, the "Promin" was created in just eight months. From 1963 it was produced at a factory in Sievierodonetsk.

The "Promin" could perform 32 different operations. The memory where the machine keeps numbers while it calculates had only 100 "cells". For comparison: a modern laptop has so many of these cells that you could never count them.

The "Promin" also worked with metallised cards — not the usual paper ones, but cards with a metal coating.

## MIR: the "machine for engineering calculations"

In 1965, Glushkov proposed a whole series of new machines. They were called MIR, from the words "machine for engineering calculations". The MIR-1 appeared that same year.

The main idea behind the MIRs was comfortable communication between a person and a machine. They could calculate with numbers of any length. And the language a person uses to "explain" to the machine what to do was built right into the machine itself. The MIR performed up to 8,000 operations a second.

Still, it was not "personal" in today's sense: the MIR-1 weighed about 400 kilograms. Your laptop is hundreds of times lighter.

## The MIR-2: a screen and a light pen

The MIR-2 appeared in 1969. It performed up to 12,000 operations a second. But the main thing was something else: it had a screen and a light pen.

So an engineer could work with the machine in a dialogue — through the screen and the light pen. For the late 1960s, that was a real wonder. The language the MIR-2 "spoke" was called "Analitik" (Analyst) — read about it in article 5.

Later the MIR-3 appeared as well. However, sources give different years for its appearance and different speeds, so historians still have work to do here.

## The IBM story

Glushkov himself recalled that in 1967, at an exhibition in London, the American company IBM bought a MIR-1. Sounds exciting! But remember: this is one person's memory, not a document. There is no other confirmation of the story so far.

Can the MIR be called the world's first personal computer? Opinions differ. Glushkov himself and the Virtual Museum call the MIR a forerunner of the personal computer. More on this in article 6.

## Fun fact

The developers of the MIR-1 received the USSR State Prize — one of the highest awards for scientists and engineers of that time.

## Bridge to today

The MIR-2's light pen is a distant ancestor of the stylus people draw with on tablets today.

## Bridge to memories

Remember doing sums on a slide rule or an early calculator. Back then, a MIR in an engineering office would have seemed like a real helper.

## Talk about it at home

- How is your computer or tablet similar to the MIR, and how is it completely different?
- How many people would it take to lift a MIR-1?
- Why is it important that a machine is easy to "talk" to?
