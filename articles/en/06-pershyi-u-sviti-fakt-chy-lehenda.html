"The First in the World"? How to Tell Fact from Legend

Where we are on the timeline: 1950–1971. This is the final article in the series. It is best read after articles 1–5.

You have probably seen online headlines like "Ukrainians created the world's first…". They are nice to read. But are they always true? Ukrainian computer history has real achievements. And that is exactly why they deserve to be described precisely — then people will trust them.

MESM: the first — but where exactly?

MESM is often described as "the first computer in continental Europe". However, from September 1950 the Z4 machine built by Zuse was already working in Zurich. So was MESM the first?

The details matter here. The Z4 ran on relays — switches that click — rather than glass tubes. And its program was not stored in the machine's memory. So the precise wording is: MESM was the first electronic machine in continental Europe with a program stored in memory.

"The first in the USSR" is confirmed by an official resolution of the Academy of Sciences in 1952. But "the first in the world" is not true: the EDSAC machine started working earlier.

Other mistakes also turn up online: that MESM had 18,000 tubes (in fact, about 6,000) or that it was launched in 1952 (in fact, on 25 December 1951).

The Address Language: one of the first

Some publications call Kateryna Yushchenko's Address Language the world's first language that a person uses to "explain" a task to a machine. Other sources are more careful. The accurate version is: one of the first in the world and the first in the USSR. That is a great achievement in itself.

The MIR: the first personal computer?

One Russian publication called the MIR the first personal computer. But the Virtual Museum of Computing in Ukraine and Viktor Glushkov himself call it a forerunner of the personal computer. And for good reason: the MIR-1 weighed about 400 kilograms.

As for the story of the American company IBM buying a MIR-1, we know it only from Glushkov's memoirs (article 4).

The "Dnipro": at the same time as the Americans

The "Dnipro" really was the first computer of its kind in the USSR. But Glushkov himself honestly admitted that a similar American machine went into production in June 1961, "at the same time as ours". So "the first in the world" does not fit here.

OGAS: a dream that did not come true

In November 1962, Glushkov was given the task of creating a system that would link the computers of the whole country. It was called OGAS. The project was developed in 1962–1964, but no decisions on it were ever taken. In 1971, OGAS was mentioned in the decisions of a Communist Party congress — yet it was never put into practice.

Many legends have grown up around OGAS. People write that the project would have cost 20 billion roubles, and that it was stopped by a secret "US operation". Neither claim is backed by documents.

Why even good sources need checking

Much of what we know about the first Ukrainian computers was recorded by Boris Malinovsky. He was not only a historian but also a participant in the events. His accounts are very valuable, but the word "first" in them is the view of an insider.

In the same way, Glushkov's memoirs are memories, not documents. They can be trusted, but it is worth remembering where they come from.

Checklist: how to check loud claims of being "first"

  1. Who is making the claim? A participant in the events, a historian, or an author who is simply retelling the story?
  2. Is there a document? A resolution, minutes, a date in an archive?
  3. Do other sources agree? If everyone else is careful and one source shouts "the first in the world", that is a reason to stop and think.
  4. First in what, exactly? First in a city, a country, Europe or the world — these are very different things.
