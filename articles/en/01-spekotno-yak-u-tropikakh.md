# Hot as the Tropics: How the First Computer Was Built Near Kyiv

> **Where we are on the timeline:** 1948–1957. This is the first article in the series. Next up: the women who worked on the first machines (article 2).

Imagine a room where six thousand glass tubes are glowing at the same time. It gets so hot that the ceiling has to be taken apart. That is how the first computer in the Soviet Union was born, just outside Kyiv. Back then, such machines were called EOM — the Ukrainian and Russian abbreviation for "electronic computing machine".

## A scientist who came to Kyiv

In 1946, Sergey Lebedev was invited to Kyiv to head the Institute of Electrical Engineering of the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences. That same year he was elected a full member of the Academy.

At the end of 1948, Lebedev took on something new: a machine that could do calculations by itself. An unusual place was found for it — a two-storey building in Feofaniya, a former monastery settlement about 10 kilometres from Kyiv. Twelve engineers worked on the machine together with Lebedev.

## A machine the size of a room

The machine was called MESM, the "Small Electronic Calculating Machine". Still, "small" is hardly the right word: it filled a hall of 60 square metres. That is about the size of a small flat.

Inside were about 6,000 glass tubes that did the job today's microchips do. Together they used 25 kilowatts of electricity. When all the tubes were switched on at once, the room turned into real "tropics". To let the heat out, the team had to take the ceiling apart.

The team worked almost without rest. Lebedev stayed in the lab for several days in a row, keeping himself going with strong tea. His colleague Zinoviy Rabinovich actually lived in the lab and went home only at weekends.

## A test where people made the mistake

In November 1950, the first working version of the machine was ready, and the team ran trial calculations on it. At the end of 1951, a commission from the USSR Academy of Sciences, led by M. Keldysh, came to Feofaniya. It spent three days testing MESM.

The first "exam" problem was about ballistics — the science of how shells fly. It was prepared by S. Krein and S. Avramenko. The machine's answer did not match the one people had worked out by hand. The team started looking for the error — and found it not in the machine, but in the hand calculation.

On 25 December 1951, MESM was put into regular use. And on 4 January 1952, the USSR Academy of Sciences officially recorded that it was the first fast electronic digital machine in the country to be brought into working order.

## What MESM calculated

In 1952, MESM was practically the only computer in the country trusted with the most important calculations. Scientists used it to study processes in thermonuclear reactions, rocket flight and space, power lines, problems in mechanics and quality control in production. Between November 1952 and July 1953, a single group of scientists carried out about 50 million operations on MESM.

There was only one MESM. It worked until 1957, then it was taken apart, and its parts were handed over to the Kyiv Polytechnic Institute.

You may have heard that MESM was "the first computer in Europe". There are some important details here, and we talk about them honestly in article 6.

## Fun fact

Viktor Glushkov, who later headed this lab, believed that the description of MESM became the country's first textbook on how computers work. He also called the lab itself the seed of the future Institute of Cybernetics.

## Bridge to today

MESM filled a whole room and did 50 operations a second. The phone in your pocket fits in your palm, yet it does incomparably more every second.

## Bridge to memories

The Feofaniya lab grew into the Institute of Cybernetics, which has been named after Viktor Glushkov since 1982. Perhaps you have heard the name from your parents, neighbours or colleagues — or maybe someone in your family worked there.

## Talk about it at home

- What would you do if your computer filled a whole room?
- Why do you think it was the people, not the machine, who made the mistake?
- Do you trust a calculator more than working it out on paper? Why?
