# How a Machine Was Taught to Understand People: the "Kyiv" Computer and the First Programming Languages

> **Where we are on the timeline:** 1954–1969. Before this: computers for a single engineer, the "Promin" and the MIR (article 4). Next: how to tell fact from legend (article 6).

A computer is very fast, but on its own it knows nothing. Every task has to be explained to it step by step. How do you do that if the machine does not understand human language? In the 1950s people in Kyiv worked on this question too — and found one of the first answers in the world.

## The "Kyiv" machine

In 1954, the Institute of Mathematics of the Ukrainian Academy of Sciences began building a new computer. Back then such machines were called EOM — "electronic computing machines". The new one was simply named "Kyiv".

The initiative came from the mathematician Boris Gnedenko. The machine was built by almost the same team that had created Kyiv's first machine, MESM. The work was led by Lev Dashevsky and Kateryna Yushchenko, and from 1956–1957 by Viktor Glushkov. We told the story of Kateryna Yushchenko in article 2.

The "Kyiv" was much more powerful than MESM. It had 2,400 glass tubes and another 15,000 smaller parts. In one second, the machine could do up to 12,000 additions — MESM managed only 50 operations. Tasks were fed into the machine from a paper tape with holes punched in it.

From 1956 to 1962, the "Kyiv" worked at the Kyiv Computing Centre. A second copy of the machine worked at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna.

## What is a programming language? Think of a recipe

Imagine writing a recipe for someone who has never been in a kitchen. "Salt to taste" will not do — they do not know what "to taste" means. You have to be precise: take a spoon, scoop half a spoonful of salt, pour it into the pan.

A computer is exactly that kind of "cook". A programming language is the language a person uses to "explain" to the machine what to do. The closer that language is to human language, the easier it is to write the "recipes" and the fewer mistakes they contain.

## The Address Language

In 1955, Kateryna Yushchenko and V. Korolyuk created the Address Language. It is considered one of the first languages of this kind in the world and the first in the USSR.

Where does the name come from? A machine keeps numbers in its memory like items in a left-luggage room. Every locker has its own number — an address. The main idea of the Address Language is not only to take a number from the right locker, but also to store the address of another locker inside one. It is like a reference in a book: "see the address on another page".

The Address Language was used not only on the "Kyiv". It was also used on other computers of the time — the M-20, the "Ural", the "Minsk" and the "Dnipro". So an idea from Kyiv worked far beyond Ukraine.

## "Analitik": a conversation with the machine

The ideas of easy communication with a computer kept developing. In 1969 came the MIR-2 — a machine for engineering calculations that we wrote about in article 4. The language people used to "talk" to it was called "Analitik" (Analyst).

An engineer worked with the MIR-2 in a dialogue: through a screen and a light pen. The machine was becoming not just a "counting device", but a conversation partner.

## Fun fact

As early as 1957, people were writing programs for logic games on the "Kyiv" machine. So computers in Ukraine were "playing" games almost seventy years ago!

## Bridge to today

A link on a website or a shortcut on your desktop works on the very same idea: "see the address somewhere else". Every time you click a link, you are using an idea that is already seventy years old.

## Bridge to memories

If you learned Basic or Pascal in a school computer class, you have already used descendants of these ideas.

## Talk about it at home

- Write a "program" — a recipe for a sandwich, step by step. Ask someone to follow it word for word. What happens if you skip one step?
- Why does a machine need such precise instructions, while a person does not?
- Where else in life do we use "addresses" to find something?

## For the classroom

- **Dates:** 1954 — work on the "Kyiv" begins; 1955 — the Address Language; 1957 — programs for logic games; 1969 — the MIR-2 and the "Analitik" language.
- **Names:** K. L. Yushchenko, V. Korolyuk, B. V. Gnedenko, L. N. Dashevsky, V. M. Glushkov.
- **Idea for a computer science lesson:** an algorithm explained through a recipe.
